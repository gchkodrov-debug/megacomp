How do you track drawdown in a prop firm challenge?

Ask funded traders what actually ends challenges and the answer is rarely a strategy. The stories repeat with different firm names: one bad day that touched a daily limit nobody had written down. A trailing floor that quietly rose behind a winning week until a normal losing day landed on it. A balance that did not match what the trader saw on his own screen — with no dated record to argue from. Almost every version of the story has the same missing piece: the numbers were never tracked on paper, so the trader was steering by a feeling about how much room was left.

A drawdown tracker fixes that, and it is simpler than most traders expect. It is not software and it is not a signal. It is five standing numbers copied out of your firm's rules, and two short writing routines — about two minutes before the session and two minutes after it. This page gives you the whole system: what the numbers are, how the daily routine runs, a worked example day with the arithmetic done in the open, the mistakes that end challenges, and the exact columns to use if you build your own sheet tonight. One boundary, stated once: this is about records only. It says nothing about what to trade, when to trade, or how to size — those decisions are yours, and no tracker makes them for you.

What does a drawdown tracker actually track?

Every challenge runs on two separate drawdown limits, and they bind independently. The daily limit is the most the account may drop in a single day. The total limit (the maximum drawdown) is the most it may fall across the whole challenge. Whichever floor the account reaches first ends the challenge — so a tracker that only follows one of the two is showing you half your position. Before any daily routine makes sense, five standing numbers have to be copied out of your firm's own documents, in the firm's own words:

  1. Starting balance — the account size you bought, exactly as the firm states it.
  2. Daily loss limit — as a percent and as a dollar figure, and what it resets from (the day-start balance, or equity).
  3. Total drawdown limit — as a percent and as a dollar figure.
  4. The mechanic — static, or trailing. If trailing: intraday or end-of-day, and whether the floor locks once it reaches the starting balance.
  5. The measurement basis — balance or equity, whether open profit and loss counts, and the hour your firm's "day" resets.

These five are facts about your firm's rulebook, not details to estimate. If the firm's documents do not answer one of them plainly, write the gap down, dated, with a note that you asked support. A dated unanswered question is also a record — and our guide on static vs trailing floors walks through the mechanic options in full.

Before the session: the two-minute setup

  1. Write the date, and the day's starting balance — from the platform, not from yesterday's memory.
  2. Carry yesterday's floor values forward.
  3. Write today's daily floor and total floor in dollars.
  4. Work out the room to each floor: current balance minus each floor.
  5. Check your two floors against the platform's own display. If they differ, that difference is a same-day screenshot for your evidence folder — not a shrug.

The smaller of the two rooms is today's real room. Everything else on the sheet is context; that one number is the position you are actually in. A number you calculated and closed is not a record; a number on the sheet is.

A worked example: Day 12 of a $100,000 challenge

Filled-in example numbers, for format — every figure invented, the layout is the point. The account: $100,000 starting balance, a 5% daily limit measured from the day-start balance, and a 10% total drawdown ($10,000) trailing end-of-day behind the highest closed balance.

  • Day 12, starting balance: 103,240
  • Daily floor: 98,078 (5% below the day's start)
  • Total floor: 93,240 (highest close so far, 103,240, minus the $10,000 limit)
  • Closing balance: 102,610 — day result −630
  • Room to the daily floor: 4,532
  • Room to the total floor: 9,370
  • Real room today: 4,532 — the daily limit binds first

Notice two things. First, the account is comfortably profitable overall and still has less than half its total room available today — because the daily limit, not the total, is the binding one. Second, notice how much clearer that is as numbers on a sheet than as a sentence in a trading diary. That clarity is the entire discipline: two floors, two rooms, smaller one wins, written before the first trade and updated after the last one.

After the session: the two-minute close

  1. Closing balance, and the day's result.
  2. The new room to each floor.
  3. Whether either floor moved. Trailing floors move on good days — record the new floor on the day it moves, not when you happen to notice.
  4. Anything that looked wrong on the platform — a balance, a rule, a price — gets a screenshot in the evidence folder tonight, with one log line: date, what it was, where it is saved.

The four tracking mistakes that end challenges

  • Tracking one limit instead of two. The daily limit and the total drawdown end challenges independently, and the one that gets you is whichever you reach first. If your sheet shows one room, it is hiding the other.
  • Being surprised by a trailing floor. Under a trailing mechanic, a winning streak moves the floor up behind you — your room can shrink while you are winning. If the floor's new value is not recorded the day it moves, the sheet is lying to you politely.
  • Not knowing whether open P&L counts. Under intraday trailing on equity, a trade that runs up and comes back can raise your floor and then touch it on the same position. Balance-based and equity-based firms behave differently while a trade is open; your five numbers have to say which one yours is.
  • Having nothing to show in a dispute. When a payout is denied or a balance is challenged, the firm has a database: dates, balances, rule versions, timestamps. If your side is a memory, the database wins — not because it is always right, but because it is the only evidence in the room. Rules snapshot on day one, balance proof at the key moments, every support exchange saved with dates, every payout request logged: that folder is the difference between knowing what happened and guessing. Build your own tracker in ten minutes

The system above needs nothing more than a notebook or a blank spreadsheet with these columns. This is the whole layout — take it freely:

  • A standing header with the five numbers, copied from your firm's documents on day one.
  • One row per day: date · starting balance · daily floor · total floor · room to daily floor · room to total floor · closing balance · day result · floor moved? (new value) · notes.
  • A payout log: date requested · amount · balance it was calculated from · what the firm said · when it arrived, or why it did not.
  • An evidence log: date · what looked wrong or changed · where the screenshot or document is saved.

Fill the header once, add one row a day, and the tracker is doing its job. Everything past this point is convenience — the same rows, pre-built, with the arithmetic done for you.

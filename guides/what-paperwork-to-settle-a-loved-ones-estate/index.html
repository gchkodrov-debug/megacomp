What paperwork do you need to settle a loved one's estate?

When someone dies, the paperwork arrives faster than the grief settles. The bank wants documents. The insurer wants different documents. The pension office wants a third set, in a particular form, with a certified copy it keeps. Meanwhile the paper lands the way it landed in the hospital week — half on the kitchen table, half still in the car, some of it in an email account nobody has the password to. Almost every family describes the same chaos, and it is worth saying plainly: the chaos is not a character flaw and it is not disrespect. It is a missing system, arriving at the worst possible moment.

This page is that system, whole: what to do in the first 48 hours, the full document hunt in one list, which office asks for what, and the one-folder method — sections, an index page, and a call log — that keeps an estate findable from the first week to the last form. One boundary, stated once: this is organisation, not legal advice. Probate, executor duties and tax rules vary by state and country, and the legal questions belong to a local estate attorney. What organisation can do — and it does a lot — is make sure no legal step ever stalls because a document could not be found.

The first 48 hours: three moves

  1. Order the death certificates — more than feels reasonable. Banks, insurers, pension providers, government offices and utilities each tend to want their own certified copy, and some will not accept a photocopy. The funeral home usually arranges them; count the institutions you will deal with, order for all of them, and add spares. Many families order ten or more. Extras ordered now cost a little; reordering mid-process costs weeks.
  2. Find and secure the core documents. The will (and any trust papers), the deed or lease, and the insurance policies. Put them in one place — today, that place can be a single folder or box — and tell one other trusted person where it is. If there was a hospital stay at the end, the discharge papers and final medication list go into the same folder: offices ask about them more often than families expect, and they are the first papers to scatter.
  3. Start the call log on day one. One page, one line per contact: date · institution · the name of the person you spoke to · what they asked for · what you sent and when · what they promised · any reference number. It feels like overkill in week one. By month two, when an office says "we never received that", the log is the difference between an argument and an answer.

The document hunt: gather these, by category

  • Identity: death certificates, birth and marriage certificates, passport, Social Security or national ID details.
  • Legal: the will, any codicils, trust documents, powers of attorney (they end at death, but offices still ask what existed), any prepaid funeral plan.
  • Money: bank and investment account statements, retirement and pension accounts, debts — cards, loans, the mortgage statement.
  • Policies: life insurance, home, vehicle, any employer-provided cover.
  • Property: deed, vehicle titles and registrations, keys and codes noted somewhere findable.
  • Taxes and work: recent tax returns, employer and pension contact details, any benefits letters.
  • The household layer: utility accounts, subscriptions, regular bills — the things that keep charging until someone tells them to stop.
  • Digital life: a list of the online accounts that exist and where access details are kept. You may not be able to open all of them, and providers have their own processes — but an estate cannot settle accounts nobody knows exist.

Gather once, into the folder, rather than hunting per-office. Every institution below will ask for some subset of this same list — that is the whole reason the hunt is worth doing in one pass.

Who asks for what

  • Banks and investment firms: a certified death certificate, proof of your authority to act (letters from the court, where required), and your own ID. Accounts are usually frozen or restricted until the paperwork clears — expect that, and do not promise anyone a date until the bank has its documents.
  • Life insurer: the death certificate, the policy number, and their claim form. If the policy cannot be found, the insurer can usually search by name and date of birth — one more reason the call log matters.
  • Employer and pension providers: the death certificate and their own forms; there may be a final pay, a pension decision, or a survivor benefit with its own deadline.
  • Government: Social Security (or your country's equivalent) needs notifying promptly — the funeral home often reports it, but confirm; benefits paid after death may have to be returned, and finding that out from the call log beats finding it out from a letter.
  • Creditors and utilities: most need only a copy of the certificate and a phone call logged properly. Keep every "account closed" confirmation in the correspondence section.
  • Property and vehicles: deeds and titles transfer through your area's own process — this is one of the places a local attorney earns their fee, because the forms differ everywhere.

The one-folder system that survives the process

You do not need software, an app, or a system to learn. One binder or folder, six sections: certificates and legal · accounts · policies · property · debts and bills · correspondence and the call log. Two habits make it work. First, an index page at the front: what is in each section, and where the originals are if the folder holds copies. Second, the same-day rule: new paper goes into its section the day it arrives, and every call gets its log line the day it happens — two minutes now instead of a reconstruction later. It is the same lesson caregiving families learn with discharge papers: one page per event, written once, in the place the next person looks. The test of the whole system is simple — could a family member who has never seen this folder find any document in two minutes, at 2 a.m., without calling you? If yes, the system is done. What organisation can't do

Settling an estate takes months, sometimes longer, and the timeline is set by courts, offices and tax rules — not by how tidy the folder is. A tidy folder cannot probate a will, value a house, or answer for a disputed claim. What it can do is remove the most common cause of delay that families actually control: the document that exists somewhere, that an office is waiting for, that nobody can find. When the legal steps themselves are unclear — and in most places some of them will be — that is the moment for a local estate attorney, folder in hand. You will pay for their answers, not for their hunting.
